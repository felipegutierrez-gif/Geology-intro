1What is a solid solution?

A mineral whose chemical recipe isn't fixed — it can swap one ingredient for a similar one across a continuous range, while keeping the same crystal structure.

Plain English

Think of coffee. Pure black coffee and a fully sweetened coffee are the "end-members." You can dial sugar anywhere in between — same cup, same drink, just a different blend. A solid solution is the same idea in a crystal: the atomic "scaffolding" stays put, but interchangeable atoms (say magnesium vs. iron) sit in the same seats in any ratio. The mineral is one continuous family, not separate species.

The rule that makes it work

Two atoms can substitute freely when they have similar size and similar charge. Mg²⁺ and Fe²⁺ are nearly the same size and identical charge, so they trade places effortlessly in olivine and pyroxene. When the swap requires balancing charge, a coupled substitution kicks in.

End-members vs. intermediates

The two pure extremes are end-members; everything between is an intermediate composition. Named varieties (like the olive-green gem peridot) are just intermediates given their own labels. Properties — color, density, melting point — shift smoothly as you move along the range.

    3Which minerals build which rocks?

    Click a mineral to see the igneous rocks it dominates — and what it pairs with.

    Plain English

    Rocks are mineral mixtures. The recipe of minerals decides the rock's name. Lots of olivine and pyroxene, no quartz → dark, dense rocks from deep down (peridotite, basalt). Lots of quartz and K-feldspar → pale, light rocks from the crust (granite). Same minerals, different cooling speed, just changes the crystal size: basalt (fast, fine) vs. gabbro (slow, coarse).

    5Bowen's Reaction Series

    The order minerals crystallize as magma cools — from hot & mafic at the top to cool & felsic at the bottom. Click any mineral.

    Plain English

    As magma cools, minerals crystallize in a predictable order. The left branch is discontinuous — each mineral reacts with the melt and converts to the next (olivine→pyroxene→amphibole→biotite). The right branch is continuous — plagioclase just gradually shifts from Ca-rich to Na-rich (a solid solution!). The two branches meet at the bottom, where the last, coolest, most silica-rich minerals form: K-feldspar, muscovite, and finally quartz.

    6Pick a rock → see its minerals

    The reverse lookup of Chapter 3. Choose a rock and see the mineral recipe that defines it, with each mineral's share shown as a bar.

    Plain English

    A rock's name is really a recipe. Geologists identify a rock by figuring out which minerals it contains and in what proportions. Quartz + K-feldspar means granite; pyroxene + Ca-plagioclase with no quartz means basalt or gabbro. Learn the recipes and you can name most igneous rocks by eye.

    7How did it cool — outside or inside the Earth?

    Pick a rock to find out whether it cooled fast at the surface in contact with air/water (extrusive / volcanic) or slowly trapped inside the crust (intrusive / plutonic) — and how to tell from the crystals.

    Extrusive (surface) Intrusive (in crust)
    Extrusive · volcanic

    Magma erupts as lava and cools in seconds-to-days against air or water. Atoms barely have time to organize, so crystals are tiny or glassy. Example: basalt (fine-grained), pumice, obsidian. Fine grain = surface.

    Intrusive · plutonic

    Magma stalls deep in the crust and cools over thousands-to-millions of years. Slow cooling grows big, visible crystals. Example: granite, gabbro, diorite. Coarse grain = depth.

    Key idea: same chemistry can give two rocks — only the cooling speed (and so the crystal size) differs. Basalt ⇄ gabbro, rhyolite ⇄ granite, andesite ⇄ diorite.

    8Metamorphic & sedimentary rocks

    Igneous rocks crystallize from melt — but they're only one third of the story. Click a process in the rock cycle, then explore example rocks below.

    Sedimentary rocks — built at the surface

    Made from broken-down bits of older rock, or from minerals/shells settling out of water. Formed by weathering → transport → deposition → burial & cementing (lithification). Click to expand.

    Metamorphic rocks — re-cooked by heat & pressure

    An existing rock is transformed in the solid state — it never melts. Heat and pressure regrow its minerals, often into aligned bands (foliation). Click to expand.

    Plain English

    Three rock families, three recipes for the same atoms. Igneous = frozen from melt. Sedimentary = glued-together debris and chemical/biological precipitates, laid down in layers at the surface. Metamorphic = an old rock cooked and squeezed until its minerals recrystallize — without ever fully melting. The rock cycle endlessly recycles material between all three.

    9What is this rock/mineral useful for?

    Pick a rock or mineral to see where it shows up in industry and everyday products — the practical payoff of all this geology.

    Plain English

    Almost everything built or manufactured starts as rock. Limestone becomes the cement in concrete; quartz sand becomes glass and silicon chips; gypsum becomes drywall; granite and marble clad buildings. Knowing a rock's minerals tells you what it's good for — hardness, chemistry, and how it breaks all decide its job.

    10Gemstones

    Gems are just minerals that happen to be rare, hard, and beautiful. Tap a card to see the mineral it comes from, how it forms, the special conditions required, and a rough price range.

    Plain English

    What turns an ordinary mineral into a gem is usually a trace of an "impurity" element plus unusual growth conditions. Pure corundum (Al₂O₃) is colorless — add a pinch of chromium and it's a ruby; add iron + titanium and it's a sapphire. Diamond needs the extreme pressure of the deep mantle; emerald needs the rare meeting of beryllium and chromium. Rarity of those conditions is what sets the price.

    Price ranges are rough retail per-carat estimates for building intuition — actual value swings enormously with size, color, clarity, and treatment.

    11Plate tectonics — the engine behind it all

    Every rock in this chapter owes its existence to moving plates. The Earth's rigid outer shell is broken into plates that drift on the hot, slowly-flowing mantle below. Where they meet, rock is born, buried, and recycled. Click a boundary or a plate on the map.

    Divergent — plates separate (➡︎⬅︎ new crust forms) Convergent — plates collide (➡︎⬅︎ crust destroyed) Transform — plates slide past

    Little arrows on each boundary show the direction of motion: facing arrows = coming together, opposing arrows = separating, offset arrows = sliding past.

    Plain English

    Picture the Earth's surface as a cracked eggshell floating on warm honey. The pieces (plates) creep about as fast as your fingernails grow. Where two pieces pull apart, molten rock wells up and freezes into new ocean floor (that's where basalt is born). Where they crash together, one usually dives under the other — melting, feeding volcanoes, and pushing up mountains. Where they just scrape past each other sideways, you get earthquakes but no new rock. Almost everything — volcanoes, mountains, earthquakes, even where gems form — traces back to these three kinds of edges.

    A few more things worth knowing

    Why the plates move

    Heat from Earth's core drives slow convection currents in the mantle. Rising hot rock and sinking cold slabs drag the plates like conveyor belts.

    Sea-floor spreading

    At mid-ocean ridges, new basalt crust forms continuously and spreads outward, recording Earth's magnetic flips in symmetric stripes — the proof that confirmed plate tectonics.

    Subduction & the Ring of Fire

    Where ocean plates dive into the mantle (subduction), they melt and feed chains of volcanoes — like the Pacific's Ring of Fire.

    Continents drift & collide

    When two continents converge, neither sinks easily, so the crust crumples upward — that's how the Himalayas (India into Asia) and the Alps were raised.

    The rock cycle's motor

    Divergent edges make igneous rock; convergent edges bury and metamorphose it; uplift and erosion feed sediment. Plate motion is what keeps Chapter 8's rock cycle turning.

    Hotspots

    Not all volcanism is at plate edges. Fixed plumes of hot mantle (hotspots) punch through moving plates, building island chains like Hawai‘i as the plate drifts overhead.
